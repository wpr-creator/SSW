His armies conquered and occupied nations — but they also carried the ideas of the French Revolution: liberty, equality, fraternity, and above all, the idea that a people could and should govern themselves. When Napoleonic troops marched into the German states, the Italian peninsula, and Poland, local populations encountered both oppression and inspiration. CHAPTER TWO

REVOLUTIONS, UNIFICATION, AND A NEW EUROPE

In 1848, nationalist and liberal uprisings erupted simultaneously across almost every major European country. In February, a revolution in Paris overthrew the French monarchy. Within weeks, revolts spread to the German states, the Austrian Empire, and the Italian peninsula. Students, workers, intellectuals, and middle-class professionals took to the streets demanding constitutions, national unity, and self-determinationThe right of people to choose their own government and decide their own political future.. For a few months, it looked as though the old order might collapse entirely. Then the monarchies regrouped, called in their armies, and crushed the revolutions one by one. By 1849, every uprising had failed. The old powers were back in control.

In 1848, uprisings broke out almost simultaneously across Europe. Revolutions swept France, Germany, Austria, and Italy. Students, workers, and professionals took to the streets demanding national unity and the right to self-government. For a few months, it seemed like the old monarchies might fall. But the kings called in their armies and crushed the revolutions one by one. By 1849, every uprising had failed. The old order had survived.

The failure of 1848 changed nationalism. Before 1848, most nationalist movements had been idealistic and liberal — they believed that all peoples deserved freedom and that nations could coexist peacefully once freed from monarchies. After 1848, nationalism became harder, more ethnic, and more willing to use force. A new word entered political vocabulary: RealpolitikGerman for "politics of reality" — the idea that nations should pursue their interests through power and strategy, not ideals., meaning politics based not on ideals but on power, strategy, and what actually works. The man who embodied this new approach most completely was Otto von Bismarck.

BismarckOtto von Bismarck, the Prussian statesman who unified Germany through military strategy and political manipulation between 1864 and 1871., as Prussia's Minister-President from 1862, set out to unify the German states not through speeches and idealism but through "blood and iron" — his own phrase. He engineered three wars in seven years: against Denmark in 1864, Austria in 1866, and France in 1870-71. Each war was carefully designed to isolate Prussia's enemy diplomatically before destroying it militarily. When France fell in 1871, Bismarck declared the German Empire in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles — choosing the French king's most magnificent palace deliberately, to humiliate France on its own soil. The new Germany was the most powerful state on the continent, and every other European power immediately had to reckon with what that meant.

CHAPTER THREE

THE BALKANS: POWDER KEG OF EUROPE

The BalkansThe peninsula in southeastern Europe — home to Serbia, Bulgaria, Bosnia, and other territories — where multiple empires and nationalist movements collided in the late 1800s and early 1900s. — the southeastern peninsula of Europe, home to Serbs, Bulgarians, Greeks, Romanians, Bosnians, and Albanians — was the region where nationalist ambitions collided most dangerously. For centuries, most of the Balkans had been ruled by the Ottoman Empire. As the Ottomans weakened through the 1800s, Balkan peoples fought for independence: Greece won its independence in 1829, Serbia gained autonomy, Bulgaria emerged as a state in 1878, and Romania unified. Each new nation became a model and an inspiration for those still under foreign rule.

The Balkans — southeastern Europe, home to Serbs, Bulgarians, Greeks, Romanians, and Bosnians — was the most dangerous collision point for nationalism in Europe. Most of the Balkans had been ruled by the Ottoman Empire for centuries. As the Ottomans weakened in the 1800s, Balkan peoples fought for independence: Greece won freedom in 1829, Serbia gained autonomy, Bulgaria emerged in 1878, Romania unified. Each new free nation inspired the next group still living under foreign rule.

The problem was that two empires still claimed large Balkan territories: the declining Ottoman Empire and the Austro-Hungarian Empire, a patchwork state that contained dozens of nationalities — Germans, Hungarians, Czechs, Slovaks, Croatians, Serbs, and more — held together by a common monarchy. Serbian nationalists dreamed of a "Greater Serbia" uniting all South Slavic peoples. Austria-Hungary saw this as an existential threat. If Serbia could pull South Slavic peoples away from the Habsburg Empire, the whole multi-ethnic structure might unravel. By 1914, the ethnic nationalismA form of nationalism based on shared ethnic identity, language, or ancestry — often used to justify redrawing borders or creating new states. of the Balkans had turned the region into what journalists of the era called "the powder keg of Europe."

Two empires still controlled parts of the Balkans. The declining Ottoman Empire held some territories. The Austro-Hungarian Empire — a patchwork of dozens of ethnic groups held together by one royal family — controlled others. Serbian nationalists dreamed of uniting all South Slavic peoples into a greater Serbia. Austria-Hungary saw this as a deadly threat: if Serbia could pull its people away, the whole multi-ethnic empire might fall apart. By 1914, the Balkans had become what journalists called "the powder keg of Europe" — a region that could ignite a world war.

CHAPTER FOUR

THE ROAD FROM SARAJEVO TO WAR

What made the assassination of Franz Ferdinand so catastrophic was not the act itself — assassinations happened frequently in this era — but the system that surrounded it. Europe in 1914 was divided into two armed alliance blocs connected by secret treaties that obligated each country to defend its allies if attacked. When Austria-Hungary blamed Serbia for the assassination and issued an ultimatum, Serbia's response triggered Austrian mobilization, which triggered Russian mobilization to protect Serbia, which triggered German mobilization to support Austria, which triggered French mobilization to honor its alliance with Russia, which triggered British mobilization to protect Belgium when Germany invaded it. In six weeks, a local act of nationalist terrorism had become a world war.

The direct thread from 1848 to 1914 is visible in hindsight. The Congress of Vienna suppressed nationalism but could not kill it. PRIMARY SOURCE MOMENT

"Germany is not looking to Prussia's liberalism for its salvation, but to its power... The great questions of the time will not be resolved by speeches and majority decisions — that was the great mistake of 1848 and 1849 — but by iron and blood."

— Otto von Bismarck, address to the Prussian parliament's Budget Committee, September 30, 1862

WHY THIS MATTERS: Bismarck is directly responding to the failure of the 1848 revolutions. He is arguing that nationalism will not be achieved through idealism, voting, or speeches — it will be achieved through military power. Notice what he calls "the great mistake of 1848": the belief that democratic methods could unify Germany. He proved his argument by fighting three wars in seven years. As you read this, consider: was Bismarck right that power works better than idealism? And what were the long-term consequences of building German national identity on that foundation?

THEN

In 1914, Sarajevo was a city where Austro-Hungarian rule met Bosnian, Serbian, and South Slavic nationalist aspirations. Gavrilo Princip stood at a corner near the Latin Bridge and fired two shots that set off a chain reaction of alliances, mobilizations, and declarations of war that killed 20 million people. The city became the place where a century of suppressed nationalism finally broke open.

NOW

Today, Sarajevo's Latin Bridge is a peaceful tourist destination and memorial site. The museum beside it tells the story of the assassination and World War I. But Sarajevo also survived its own nationalist siege in the 1990s, when Serbian nationalist forces surrounded the city for nearly four years during the Bosnian War — the longest siege of a capital city in modern history. The same tensions nationalism created in 1914 reappeared in 1992.

CONNECTION

The nationalism that the Congress of Vienna tried to suppress in 1815, that erupted in 1848, that unified Germany and Italy by 1871, that blew up the world in 1914, and that tore Yugoslavia apart in the 1990s is the same force — the belief that people who share an ethnic or cultural identity deserve their own state, at whatever cost. It has built countries and destroyed them. The question it keeps forcing is the same one it forced on Gavrilo Princip: how far is it right to go for the nation you believe in?

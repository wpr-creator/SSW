CHAPTER ONE

THE TREATY OF VERSAILLES: GERMANY PAYS THE BILL

The leaders of France, Britain, and the United States met in the Palace of Versailles outside Paris in January 1919. Germany was not invited. The winners of the war were going to decide everything.

The leaders of France, Britain, and the United States met near Paris in January 1919. Germany was not invited. The winners of the war made the decisions.

France wanted Germany punished. Germany had invaded France twice in fifty years. A French general said the treaty was not real peace. He said it was only a twenty-year pause. He was almost exactly right.

CHAPTER TWO

WILSON'S DREAM: FOURTEEN POINTS VS. WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED

American President Woodrow Wilson arrived in Paris with a plan. He called it the Fourteen Points. It was his vision for a peaceful world after the war. Wilson believed that if nations treated each other fairly, there would be no reason to fight again.

President Woodrow Wilson came to Paris with a plan. He called it the Fourteen Points. He wanted a fair peace. He believed fair treatment could prevent another war.

One of his most important ideas was self-determinationThe idea that people of the same culture or nationality should be allowed to choose their own government and borders.. Wilson believed every group of people should have the right to form their own country. No empire should rule over people who did not want to be ruled.

One key idea was self-determination. This means people should help choose their own government and borders. Wilson said empires should not rule people who did not want to be ruled.

But Wilson's Fourteen Points ran into a hard wall in Paris. France and Britain had their own goals. They wanted colonies, territory, and revenge. When the final treaty was written, most of Wilson's ideals were weakened or removed entirely.

But Wilson's plan did not survive Paris. France and Britain wanted revenge, land, and colonies. Many of Wilson's ideas were weakened or removed.

The one idea Wilson refused to give up was the League of NationsAn international organization created after World War I to solve disputes and prevent future wars.. He believed that if nations joined together and solved problems peacefully, war could be avoided forever. It was a powerful idea. It just had one enormous problem.

Wilson refused to give up on the League of Nations. This was a group of countries meant to solve problems peacefully. Wilson thought it could stop future wars. But it had one huge problem.

The United States never joined. Wilson brought the treaty home and asked the Senate to approve it. The Senate said no. Some senators worried the League would drag America into foreign wars. Others did not want Wilson to win a political victory. The final vote failed twice.

WHY DID THE U.S. SENATE SAY NO?

Senator Henry Cabot Lodge led the opposition. He believed Congress, not the League, should decide when America went to war. Many Americans agreed. After losing 116,000 soldiers in a war that felt like Europe's problem, they wanted to stay out of international commitments.

CHAPTER FOUR

A BROKEN WORLD: ANGER, DEBT, AND SHATTERED TRUST

By 1920, the world that existed before the war was gone. Four empires had collapsed: the German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian, and Ottoman empires. New countries appeared on the map. Old certainties were destroyed. Millions of people felt lied to.

By 1920, the old world was gone. Four empires had collapsed. New countries appeared. Many people no longer trusted their leaders.

Germany's economy collapsed under the weight of reparations. By 1923, German money was worth almost nothing. A loaf of bread cost billions of marks. People burned banknotes for heat because it was cheaper than buying firewood. Middle-class families who had saved money their whole lives were wiped out overnight.

Germany's economy collapsed. By 1923, German money was almost worthless. Bread cost billions of marks. Some people burned money for heat because it was cheaper than wood. Families lost savings they had built for years.

Young soldiers came home and found few jobs and little stability. They had watched friends die. Leaders could not explain why it had been worth it. Many people felt betrayed.

The old institutions Europeans had trusted before the war now looked weak. Emperors were gone. Democratic governments seemed unable to solve the crisis. Into that empty space, dangerous political movements began to grow. They promised strength, pride, and revenge. Germany was listening.

Many people lost trust in old institutions. Emperors were gone. Democracies looked weak. New political movements promised pride and revenge. Many Germans listened.

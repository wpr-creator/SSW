World History — Semester 1, Lesson 05

Causes of World War I

Essential Question: How did rivalry, nationalism, and one assassination pull the entire world into war?

On June 28, 1914, a nineteen-year-old named Gavrilo Princip was standing in a crowd in Sarajevo, Bosnia, holding a pistol. He had already failed once that morning. The motorcade carrying Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria had taken a different route after an earlier bomb attack, and Gavrilo had given up and stopped at a deli to eat a sandwich. Then, by accident, the Archduke's car took a wrong turn and stopped directly in front of him.

Gavrilo stepped forward and fired twice. Within six weeks, the most powerful nations on earth were at war. Within four years, more than 20 million people were dead. It is one of the most important questions in all of history: how did the shooting of one man in a European street set the entire world on fire?

■ ■ ■

M.A.I.N.: Why Europe Was Already a Powder Keg

The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand pulled the trigger, but the gun had been loaded for decades. Historians use the acronym M.A.I.N. to name the four forces that had been building tension in Europe since the 1870s. Each one made war more likely. Together, they made it almost unavoidable.

M
Militarism

MilitarismThe belief that a country should build up a large, powerful military and be ready and willing to use it — often leading to an arms race between rival nations. means building up your military and making it the center of national pride. Between 1870 and 1914, Germany's army grew from 400,000 to over 2 million soldiers. Britain responded by expanding its navy. France rebuilt after losing to Germany in 1871. Every major power was in an arms race, spending enormous sums on weapons, ships, and soldiers. When you spend that much on an army, there is pressure to use it.

A
Alliance System

Europe had divided into two armed camps. The Triple Alliance linked Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy. The Triple Entente linked France, Russia, and Britain. An allianceA formal agreement between countries to help and defend each other, especially in times of war. means: if one country goes to war, all of its partners go to war too. This system was supposed to keep the peace by making war too costly. Instead, it meant that a small conflict between two countries could instantly drag in six or more.

I
Imperialism

As we saw in the last lesson, European powers had been competing aggressively for colonies across Africa and Asia. By 1900, that competition had created deep resentments and near-wars. Germany felt left out of the colonial scramble; France and Britain had empires and Germany wanted one. Economic rivalry over colonies was creating diplomatic crises every few years, each one pushing Europe closer to open conflict.

N
Nationalism

NationalismThe strong belief that people who share a culture, language, or history should govern themselves — and that your nation is special and worth fighting for. was the most explosive force of all. In the Balkans region of southeastern Europe, dozens of ethnic groups wanted their own independent nations. Serbia wanted to unite all South Slavic peoples under one flag. Austria-Hungary, which controlled millions of Slavic people, was terrified of that idea. This collision between Serbian nationalism and Austro-Hungarian fear of collapse was the specific spark that started the war.

Europe in 1914, showing the two armed alliance systems. The Triple Alliance (Central Powers) in one color; the Triple Entente (Allied Powers) in another. Any war between two members would automatically pull in all the others. Wikimedia Commons.

These four forces did not exist in isolation. They fed each other. Militarism made alliances feel necessary; alliances made military buildups feel urgent; imperial competition created the rivalries that made both seem reasonable; and nationalism made every dispute feel like a matter of survival and honor rather than a problem to be negotiated. Europe in 1914 was not just tense. It was structurally designed to explode. All it needed was a match.

Today, when you hear about NATO allies debating whether to send troops when one member is attacked, you are watching the same alliance logic that turned Sarajevo into a world war. The question of when an alliance obligation requires countries to fight is still the most dangerous question in international relations.

"I no longer recognize parties; I recognize only Germans."

Kaiser Wilhelm II of Germany, speech to the German Reichstag, August 4, 1914 — the day Germany declared war

Wilhelm said this to announce that political divisions inside Germany no longer mattered; once war began, every German was expected to unite behind the nation — this is nationalism operating at full power, and it worked: the German Reichstag voted unanimously for war funding that same day.

Sarajevo: The Shot That Started It All

Left: Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria-Hungary, heir to the throne, whose assassination in Sarajevo on June 28, 1914, triggered the war. Right: Gavrilo Princip, 19 years old, photographed at his trial in 1914. Wikimedia Commons.

Gavrilo Princip was a member of a secret Serbian nationalist group called the Black Hand. The group wanted to drive Austria-Hungary out of Bosnia and unite Slavic peoples under Serbian leadership. They had planned the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand for months. Franz Ferdinand was the heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne and was visiting Sarajevo, the capital of Bosnia; a region Austria-Hungary controlled but where many people felt loyalty to Serbia.

The day did not go smoothly for the assassins. A group of Black Hand members had positioned themselves along the motorcade route. The first attempt, a bomb thrown at the car, bounced off and exploded under the car behind it. Franz Ferdinand arrived at City Hall furious. He changed his plans to visit the injured officers in the hospital. His driver, unfamiliar with the new route, took a wrong turn and stalled the car directly in front of Gavrilo Princip, who was standing at the corner. Princip fired twice at close range. Franz Ferdinand and his wife Sophie were both mortally wounded. They died within the hour.

Latin Bridge, Sarajevo, the site where Gavrilo Princip fired the shots that killed Archduke Franz Ferdinand, June 28, 1914. Wikimedia Commons.

Austria-Hungary blamed Serbia for the assassination. They sent Serbia a list of ten demands so extreme that historians believe Austria-Hungary did not expect Serbia to accept them; the point was to create a reason for war. Serbia accepted nine of the ten demands. Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia anyway on July 28, 1914: exactly one month after the assassination.

Why did Austria-Hungary want war with Serbia? They feared that Serbian nationalism would inspire the many ethnic groups inside their own empire to demand independence. If Serbia became a powerful, independent Slavic nation, it might pull apart the entire Austro-Hungarian Empire from within. From Austria-Hungary's point of view, crushing Serbia was not aggression. It was self-defense.

This pattern: a smaller conflict, framed as self-defense, used to justify a larger war: has repeated many times in history since 1914. Recognizing that pattern is one of the most important skills a student of history can have.

The Dominoes Fall: Six Weeks in July and August 1914

German soldiers boarding trains to the front, August 1914. Many believed the war would be over by Christmas. They were wrong by four years and 20 million deaths. Wikimedia Commons.

Once Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia, the alliance system took over like a machine that no one could stop. Russia began to mobilizeTo prepare and organize a military for war; mobilization means calling up soldiers, moving troops, and getting weapons and supplies ready to fight. its army to defend Serbia, its Slavic ally. Germany warned Russia to stop. Russia refused. Germany declared war on Russia on August 1, 1914.

Germany then declared war on France, Russia's ally, on August 3. To attack France quickly, Germany followed a plan called the Schlieffen Plan, which required marching through neutral Belgium. When German troops crossed the Belgian border, Britain declared war on Germany on August 4, 1914. Britain had a treaty obligation to defend Belgian neutrality. Within six weeks of a single assassination, all of Europe's major powers were at war.

June 28, 1914

Archduke Franz Ferdinand and his wife Sophie are assassinated in Sarajevo by Gavrilo Princip.

July 23, 1914

Austria-Hungary sends Serbia an ultimatum with ten demands. Serbia accepts nine; Austria-Hungary rejects the response.

July 28, 1914

Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia. Russia begins mobilizing its army to defend Serbia.

August 1, 1914

Germany declares war on Russia. France begins full military mobilization.

August 3, 1914

Germany declares war on France and invades neutral Belgium using the Schlieffen Plan.

August 4, 1914

Britain declares war on Germany. The world is now at war. The United States will join in 1917.

The speed of this collapse shocked even the leaders who caused it. German Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg reportedly asked his aide, after it was all in motion: "How did it all happen?" His aide answered: "If only we knew." The alliance system had taken on a life of its own. Each country's mobilization forced the next country to mobilize faster or risk being caught unprepared. No single leader chose World War I. They all stumbled into it together.

Selling the War: Propaganda and the Home Front

Left: British recruitment poster featuring Lord Kitchener, 1914 — one of the most imitated images in poster history. Right: American poster featuring Uncle Sam by James Montgomery Flagg, 1917. Both use the same technique: pointing directly at the viewer to create personal obligation. Wikimedia Commons / Library of Congress.

Once war began, every government needed to do two things: fill its armies with volunteers, and keep the civilian population supportive of the war effort. They used propagandaInformation — often exaggerated, one-sided, or misleading — that a government or group spreads to persuade people to support a cause or believe a particular view. to do both. Propaganda is not always lies; it is always selective. It shows you what the government wants you to see and hides what it does not.

German WWI propaganda poster, circa 1914-1918. Every nation used propaganda to motivate soldiers and civilian workers and to demonize the enemy. Wikimedia Commons.

WWI governments used several standard propaganda techniques. Demonization painted the enemy as monsters, beasts, or less than human. Duty and shame posters showed soldiers leaving for the front and asked civilians: what are you doing while they fight? Fear posters warned that if your country lost, terrible things would happen to your family. Pride posters connected fighting to national identity: a real Frenchman fights; a true Briton enlists.

These techniques worked. In Britain, roughly 2.5 million men volunteered in the first year of the war: before conscription even began. Young men were shamed into enlisting by women who handed white feathers to any man not in uniform. The propaganda machine was so effective that for the first two years of the war, most citizens in every country genuinely believed their nation was fighting a just, necessary, and winnable war. The reality of the trenches eventually changed that belief; but by then, the machine was too large to stop.

Today, every government that goes to war uses propaganda techniques that would be immediately recognized by a WWI poster designer. The specific images change; the emotional appeals to duty, fear, pride, and hatred of the enemy stay almost identical. Recognizing these techniques in real time is one of the most valuable skills a citizen can have.

"The lamps are going out all over Europe; we shall not see them lit again in our lifetime."

Sir Edward Grey, British Foreign Secretary, spoken to a friend on the night of August 3, 1914, as Britain prepared to enter the war

Grey said this as he watched the gas streetlamps being lit outside his window in London — he meant it both literally and as a metaphor: the civilization Europe had built was about to be destroyed, and he knew it even as the crowds outside were cheering.

■ Local History: Camp Kearny and San Diego's World War I Mobilization

Camp Kearny, located on what is now Marine Corps Air Station Miramar, San Diego, 1917-1918. At its peak, more than 40,000 soldiers trained here before shipping to Europe. Library of Congress / Wikimedia Commons.

When the United States entered World War I in April 1917, the military needed to train hundreds of thousands of soldiers almost overnight. Camp Kearny, built on a mesa north of San Diego in just a few months in 1917, became one of the largest military training installations in the country. At its peak, more than 40,000 soldiers were stationed there at once, most of them drawn from California, Arizona, New Mexico, and Utah.

The same mobilization process described in this lesson: the rapid conversion of civilian men into soldiers, the construction of massive military infrastructure, the propaganda campaign that pushed men to enlist: played out in real time on what is now the land surrounding Miramar. San Diego's identity as a military city began during World War I, and it has never stopped. The decision made in Berlin and Sarajevo in the summer of 1914 reached all the way to a mesa in Southern California three years later.

■ ■ ■
Alliance obligations, nationalist movements, and arms races are not history. They are today's front pages.

NATO is a military alliance. If Russia attacks a NATO member, every other NATO country is legally obligated to defend it. That is the same logic that turned a Serbian assassination into a world war in 1914. Nationalist movements are reshaping borders in Europe, Asia, and the Middle East right now. Arms races between the United States, Russia, and China are producing weapons far more powerful than anything a WWI general could have imagined. The M.A.I.N. causes are still running.

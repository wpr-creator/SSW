CHAPTER ONE

THE REVEAL: WHAT MADE IT POSSIBLE

The iPhone was only possible because of two decades of globalizationThe process by which the world's economies became deeply connected through trade, technology, and the movement of goods, capital, and people. that had already reorganized how things were made. When China joined the World Trade OrganizationAn international organization that sets rules for trade between countries and works to lower barriers to buying and selling across borders. in 2001, it opened the world's largest workforce to global corporations. Apple could design products in California, manufacture components in Taiwan, South Korea, and Japan, assemble the finished device in China, and sell it everywhere — all within a single integrated system.

FoxconnA Taiwanese electronics manufacturing company that assembles Apple products and employs over one million workers across China., the Taiwanese company that assembled iPhones, built enormous factory campuses in Shenzhen that were essentially self-contained cities. Workers lived in dormitories, ate in company cafeterias, and worked twelve-hour shifts. At its peak, the Shenzhen campus employed over 350,000 workers. They earned roughly $1.50 to $2.00 per hour. The precision required was extraordinary: assembling an iPhone required 141 separate steps, and workers performed the same motions thousands of times each shift. When Apple launched a new model, Foxconn could scale from prototype to mass production in weeks — a speed no factory in the United States could have matched.

Apple kept its design, software, and marketing in California — the highest-paying, highest-skill parts of the business. Everything else was distributed around the world to wherever it could be done most cheaply. This model — called offshoringMoving parts of a company's production to another country, usually to take advantage of lower labor costs. — was not unique to Apple. But Apple perfected it. By keeping its most valuable work at home and moving everything else overseas, Apple captured most of the profit from a device built by workers earning wages that no American worker would accept.

DID YOU KNOW

Artisanal coltan miners, Democratic Republic of Congo. Source: Wikimedia Commons.

Inside every smartphone is a mineral called coltan — short for columbite-tantalite — that makes the capacitors work. The Democratic Republic of Congo holds roughly 80 percent of the world's known coltan reserves. Much of it is mined by hand, in dangerous conditions, by workers earning a few dollars a day. The country sitting on one of the world's most valuable technology minerals is also one of the world's poorest. The iPhone that sold for $499 at launch contained materials that cost the people who dug them out of the ground almost nothing.

CHAPTER TWO

BEFORE IT REACHES YOU: THE SUPPLY CHAIN

Tracing a single iPhone from raw materials to finished product means traveling through at least a dozen countries. It begins underground. The cobalt in the battery comes largely from the Democratic Republic of Congo, where an estimated 40,000 children work in artisanal mines in dangerous conditions. Lithium comes from salt flats in Bolivia and Chile. Tungsten, used in the vibration motor, comes from mines in China, Rwanda, and Australia. These rare earth mineralsA group of metals found in small quantities around the world that are essential for electronics, magnets, and batteries. are the invisible foundation of the digital economy.

From there, the components travel through a supply chainThe full network of suppliers, manufacturers, shippers, and sellers that move a product from raw material to customer. spanning multiple continents. The processor chip — the brain of the device — is designed by Apple in California but fabricated by TSMC in Taiwan, using equipment made in the Netherlands. The glass screen is made by Corning in Kentucky, then chemically strengthened and shipped to China. Samsung in South Korea supplies the memory chips. The finished components arrive at Foxconn in Shenzhen, where workers assemble them into a finished device. That device then travels by container ship across the Pacific to a warehouse in the United States, then to a store, then into your pocket.

The entire system was designed for efficiency and low cost — not for the wellbeing of the people inside it. Apple earned a profit of roughly $200 to $400 on every iPhone sold. The workers who assembled it earned less than $20 for the labor that went into each device. The miners who extracted the cobalt earned perhaps a few cents per phone's worth of material. The global supply chain created an iPhone that was remarkable and affordable. It did so by distributing costs and benefits in a way that was not even close to equal.

CHAPTER THREE

WHAT IT UNLEASHED: THE ECONOMY THAT FOLLOWED

When Apple launched the App Store in 2008, it created something that had never existed before: a global marketplace for software that anyone, anywhere, could access instantly. Within two years, the App Store had 100,000 applications. A developer in Lagos could write an app and sell it to a user in Oslo. A farmer in rural Kenya could download a program that told him commodity prices before he decided whether to sell his crop. A teenager in Jakarta could build a business. The smartphone did not just connect people to information — it connected people to economic opportunity in ways that no previous technology had reached.

When Apple launched the App Store in 2008, it created a global marketplace for software that anyone could access anywhere. Within two years it had 100,000 apps. A developer in Nigeria could sell an app to a user in Norway. A farmer in Kenya could check prices before selling his crops. A teenager in Indonesia could build a business from a phone. The smartphone connected people to economic opportunity in ways no previous technology had managed.

But the economy the smartphone created had a shadow side that its designers did not fully anticipate. Every app a person used collected data: location, habits, preferences, relationships, movements, purchases, and emotions. That data was the raw material of a new industry — sometimes called surveillance capitalismAn economic system in which companies collect detailed data about users' behavior and sell it to advertisers, often without users' full understanding or consent. — in which the product being sold was not the phone or the app, but the user's attention and personal information. Facebook, Google, and hundreds of smaller companies built trillion-dollar businesses on information that users generated for free, often without understanding what they were giving away.

Authoritarian governments saw the same technology and recognized a different opportunity. China built a national mobile surveillance system that used smartphones as tracking devices, linked to facial recognition cameras and social credit monitoring. Governments in Russia, Iran, Belarus, and elsewhere monitored protesters through their phones, arrested activists based on their digital activity, and shut down mobile networks to suppress uprisings. The device that opened the world also gave states new tools to close it back down — selectively, precisely, and at a scale that no previous government had been able to achieve.

Liu Bolin began this series in 2005 after the Chinese government demolished his Beijing art studio without compensation or notice. He called the work a response to feeling invisible — erased by the system around him. When he camouflages himself against walls of electronics and consumer goods, he is not celebrating the products. CHAPTER FOUR

WHO WON, WHO DIDN'T

Apple's rise along the globalized supply chain produced one of the most dramatic concentrations of wealth in corporate history. In 2018, Apple became the first company in history to be valued at one trillion dollars. By 2022, its valuation had reached nearly three trillion dollars — more than the entire GDP of France. Its founders and early investors became among the wealthiest people who had ever lived. The engineers, designers, and executives who worked at Apple's California headquarters earned median salaries of over $200,000 per year.

This gap is not an accident or an oversight. It is the structure of the global economy as it was designed. The countries and people at the bottom of supply chains — those who extract raw materials or perform assembly labor — capture the least value. The companies and workers at the top — those who own the intellectual property, the brand, and the software — capture the most. Globalization created enormous total wealth. The question it could not answer on its own was how that wealth would be shared. The answer, so far, has been: unequally, in ways that reflect and reinforce the existing gaps between the powerful and the powerless.

THEN

On June 29, 2007, the first iPhone went on sale. Customers lined up for days outside Apple Stores across the United States. The device sold out in hours. It was celebrated as a triumph of American innovation — designed in California, marketed as a miracle, priced at $499. Almost nothing in that celebration mentioned the supply chain that made it possible, the workers in Shenzhen who assembled it, or the miners in Congo who dug the materials from the ground.

NOW

Today, workers and communities throughout the iPhone supply chain are organizing to demand a fairer share of the wealth they help create. In the DRC, mining cooperatives and international advocacy organizations are pushing technology companies to source minerals ethically and pay fair prices. In China, labor activists have documented conditions inside Foxconn facilities and pushed for better wages and overtime limits. Some companies — under pressure from consumers, investors, and regulators — have begun publishing supply chain audits and committing to eliminate child labor from their mineral sourcing. Progress is slow, but the pressure is real.

CONNECTION

The iPhone made the globalized economy visible in a way no textbook description could. It showed that the things people use every day are built on a network that stretches across dozens of countries and involves millions of people whose names and lives never appear on the product. The workers at the bottom of that network are now demanding to be seen — and the question of whether globalization's benefits can be redistributed more fairly is one of the central political and economic arguments of the 21st century.

If the true cost of building a smartphone fairly — paying miners, factory workers, and assemblers wages they could actually live on — raised the retail price significantly, would that change how you think about buying one? What do you think a company owes the people who make its products?

CHAPTER ONE

A NEW ECONOMIC ORDER

Before World War II was even over, the major Allied powers gathered in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire in 1944 to design the economic system that would govern the postwar world. They had watched the 1930s Great Depression ripple catastrophically from country to country through unregulated global trade, and they were determined to prevent it from happening again. The Bretton WoodsA 1944 international agreement that created the World Bank, IMF, and a new framework for global trade and currency. agreements created three major institutions: the World Bank to fund reconstruction and development, the IMFInternational Monetary Fund — an organization that provides financial support and stability to countries in economic crisis. (International Monetary Fund) to stabilize currencies and help countries in financial crisis, and what would become the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) to lower barriers to international trade.

Before World War II ended, leaders of the Allied nations met in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire in 1944 to design the global economic system. They wanted to prevent another Great Depression, which had spread from country to country through unregulated trade in the 1930s. They created the World Bank to fund reconstruction and development, the IMF to help countries in financial crisis, and a trade agreement to reduce barriers to international commerce. These institutions still shape the world economy today.

The system was built around American economic dominance. The U.S. dollar became the world's reserve currency — the money other countries used to trade with each other and hold as savings. This gave the United States enormous advantages: it could print money, borrow cheaply, and set the terms of global trade. In the following decades, multinational corporationsCompanies that operate in many countries at once, manufacturing in low-wage countries and selling products globally. based largely in the United States, Europe, and Japan began manufacturing goods in low-wage developing countries and selling them globally. A shoe made in Vietnam, assembled in Taiwan, sold in Ohio — this supply chain model became the default structure of the global economy.

The oil shocks of 1973 and 1979 cracked the system's confidence. When oil prices quadrupled, inflation spread globally, growth stalled, and governments realized how dependent their entire industrial economies were on a single commodity controlled by a small group of countries. InterdependenceWhen countries' economies are so connected that what happens in one directly affects the others. — the quality of being so economically connected that a crisis anywhere becomes a crisis everywhere — was no longer an abstract concept. It was a gas line.

DID YOU KNOW

The first email was sent in 1971 — a full 20 years before the internet was available to the public. The programmer who sent it, Ray Tomlinson, later admitted he couldn't remember what it said. He chose the @ symbol to separate usernames from computer addresses because it wasn't commonly used in names and would cause no confusion. That single design decision — choosing @ — is still in every email address on earth today.

CHAPTER TWO

SCREENS CHANGE EVERYTHING

Television changed the world before the internet did. In the 1960s and 1970s, television brought images of war, famine, and revolution into living rooms for the first time — and changed what governments could hide from their own people. Americans watching the Vietnam War on the evening news saw something different from what the government was telling them. Viewers watching footage of civil rights protesters being beaten in Selma, Alabama, in 1965 responded differently than readers of newspaper accounts. Images moved people in ways that words alone did not. The term "the living room war" was coined for Vietnam because for the first time, combat was broadcast into family homes in real time.

The personal computer revolution of the 1980s moved information from institutions to individuals. Before the PC, computers were enormous, expensive machines owned by governments, universities, and large corporations. The IBM Personal Computer, released in 1981, and the Apple Macintosh, released in 1984, brought computing into offices and homes. By the end of the decade, businesses, schools, and households around the world were being reorganized around desktop computers. Work that required a room full of clerks could now be done by one person with a spreadsheet. The technologyTools and systems — especially computers and the internet — that transform how information is created, shared, and used. was changing not just what people did, but how they thought about information itself.

Satellite technology accelerated the transformation. CNN launched in 1980 as the world's first 24-hour news network — and proved in 1991 during the Gulf War that it was possible to broadcast a war live as it happened. By the time the Soviet Union collapsed in 1991, satellite dishes were poking up from rooftops in Eastern Europe and across the developing world, pulling in signals from beyond any government's ability to fully control. Information was becoming harder to suppress — which was both a tool for democracy and, as governments would eventually discover, a tool for something more complicated.

CHAPTER THREE

THE INTERNET ECONOMY

When the internet became publicly available in the early 1990s, it did not just create new communication tools — it created a new kind of economy. Capital could now move across borders instantly. A company in New York could manage a supply chain that touched factories in 12 countries simultaneously. A designer in São Paulo could sell to a customer in Tokyo without a middleman. Information — which had always been difficult and expensive to move — became essentially free to transmit. This fundamentally changed what was valuable: not physical products, but the knowledge, code, and data that organized their production.

China's rise as a global manufacturing hub was the defining economic story of this period. After Deng Xiaoping's market reforms in the 1980s, China opened its enormous workforce — hundreds of millions of people willing to work for wages far below what American or European workers required — to multinational corporations. When China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001, the process accelerated dramatically. Between 2000 and 2010, the United States lost approximately 5.5 million manufacturing jobs — a transformation that restructured entire regions, devastated factory towns, and fueled political anger that reshaped American politics for decades. Meanwhile, hundreds of millions of Chinese citizens were lifted out of poverty in what economists called the largest reduction in poverty in human history.

The internet also gave authoritarian governments powerful new tools. SurveillanceThe monitoring and tracking of people's behavior, communications, and movements — increasingly done digitally by governments and corporations. technology — cameras, facial recognition systems, social media monitoring, and mobile tracking — allowed governments to watch their citizens at a scale that no previous authoritarian system had been able to achieve. China built what became known as the "Great Firewall" — a national internet filtering system blocking foreign websites and monitoring domestic content. But ordinary people also gained tools: smartphones and social media allowed citizens to organize movements, share video of abuses, and coordinate protests faster than governments could respond, as the Arab Spring uprisings of 2010 and 2011 demonstrated.

ART SPOTLIGHT

Warhol deliberately chose Campbell's Soup because he had eaten it for lunch every day for 20 years. The choice was not random — it was a statement: the most "ordinary" mass-produced product in America, repeated 32 times, could be art. When globalization later moved the manufacturing of those soup cans to facilities across multiple countries, Warhol's celebration of American mass production became unintentionally a portrait of a world economy that was about to be restructured beyond recognition.

CHAPTER FOUR

WINNERS AND LOSERS

The promise of free tradeTrade between countries with few or no government restrictions, tariffs, or barriers. was simple: if countries specialized in what they produced most efficiently and traded freely, everyone would benefit. The reality was more complicated. Global trade did create enormous wealth — but it was captured unequally. Corporations and their shareholders benefited. Consumers in wealthy countries benefited from cheaper goods. Hundreds of millions of workers in developing countries got factory jobs that, while difficult, paid more than subsistence farming. But workers in American and European manufacturing towns lost their jobs, their industries, and sometimes their communities — without the retraining, safety nets, or support to transition to new livelihoods. The gains from globalization were real. So was the damage, and it was not distributed equally.

The promise of free trade — trading without barriers between countries — was that everyone would benefit. The reality was more complicated. Global trade created enormous wealth, but it wasn't shared equally. Corporations and shareholders won. Consumers in rich countries got cheaper goods. Factory workers in developing countries got jobs. But workers in American and European manufacturing towns lost their jobs and their communities without support to find new ones. The gains from globalization were real. So was the damage — and the damage fell hardest on people who had the least power.

The information economy created similar patterns of unequal gain. Companies like Apple, Google, Amazon, and Meta became among the most valuable corporations in history. Their founders and early investors became extraordinarily wealthy. But the workers who physically assembled devices in factories, drove delivery trucks, or moderated content online were often paid low wages with few benefits, their work deliberately classified to avoid labor protections. Meanwhile, the data that users generated for free — every search, purchase, and social media post — was being collected, analyzed, and sold as the raw material of a new industry worth trillions of dollars. Users were, in a very real sense, the product.

By the early 2000s, globalization had become one of the most contested ideas in world politics. At the 1999 World Trade Organization meeting in Seattle, tens of thousands of protesters — labor unions, environmental groups, and human rights advocates from around the world — shut down the meeting and confronted police in what became known as the "Battle of Seattle." The protests represented something real: that the benefits of economic integration were not reaching everyone, that the institutions managing globalization were not accountable to those most affected by their decisions, and that the question of who benefits and who bears the cost was not being honestly answered.

PRIMARY SOURCE MOMENT

"The original design of the Web was that it should be a universal space... The fact that you can link to any piece of information and there are no boundaries — that was the exciting thing. The dream was to make it a medium that would allow people to work together, across distance and across disciplines... What I did not anticipate was the way in which it would also become a medium for misinformation, for hate speech, for surveillance, for manipulation."

— Tim Berners-Lee, inventor of the World Wide Web, reflecting on the internet's development, 2019

WHY THIS MATTERS: Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web in 1991 and gave it away for free — he deliberately did not patent it, because he wanted it to be accessible to everyone. He is describing the gap between what the internet was designed to be and what it became. As you read this, consider: what does it mean that the person who invented the most transformative communication technology in history did not fully anticipate its consequences? And given what you know about how the internet has changed the world — for better and worse — how would you respond to him?

THEN

In the 1990s, internet access required a desktop computer, a phone line, and a subscription. It was slow, expensive, and available mostly to people in wealthy countries. The excitement was real: for the first time, anyone with access could publish, communicate, and access information globally. But "anyone with access" excluded most of the world.

NOW

Today more than 4.9 billion people use the internet — most through smartphones that are more powerful than the computers that ran NASA's moon missions. Farmers in Kenya check commodity prices before selling crops. Students in rural India attend classes online. Activists in authoritarian countries share footage of abuses before governments can remove it. The internet became the infrastructure of daily life for more than half the human population.

CONNECTION

The spread of the internet fulfilled part of its original promise — connecting people across distance and giving individuals access to information that was once controlled by institutions. But it also enabled mass surveillance, the rapid spread of misinformation, the concentration of enormous wealth in a handful of tech companies, and new forms of manipulation that the people who built it did not design and could not prevent. The information revolution is still happening. Its final shape is not yet clear.

CHAPTER ONE

THE WORLD BECOMES A FACTORY

The iPhone was possible because the world economy had changed before it arrived. In the late 20th century, governments lowered trade barriers, shipping became cheaper, and corporations learned to divide production across many countries. China became especially important after it joined the World Trade OrganizationAn international group that sets trade rules and encourages countries to lower barriers to trade. in 2001. This gave global companies access to a huge workforce and powerful factory networks. A product could now be designed in one country, assembled in another, and sold almost everywhere.

The iPhone was possible because the world economy had already changed. Governments lowered trade barriers. Shipping became cheaper. Companies learned to divide production across many countries. This system changed world history because it made distance matter less. A corporation no longer needed to build every part of a product near its headquarters. It could search the world for the lowest costs, the fastest factories, the best parts, and the biggest consumer markets. This created cheaper goods and enormous profits. It also made workers in different countries compete against each other for jobs, wages, and contracts.

This system changed world history because distance mattered less than before. A company did not need to build every part near its headquarters. Apple did not invent this system, but it used it better than almost any company. The company kept design, branding, and software in California because those parts created the highest value. It used offshoringMoving work to another country, often because labor or production costs are lower there. for much of the manufacturing and assembly. The phone became a symbol of innovation, but it also became a symbol of a world where production was split into many invisible steps. The customer saw one clean object; history shows the network behind it.

Stacks of standardized shipping containers at a port. Source: Wikimedia Commons.

A shipping container looks boring, but it helped change the world. Because containers are the same basic size, they can move from ship to train to truck without unpacking the goods inside. That simple box helped make global supply chains fast enough for companies to build products across oceans.

CHAPTER TWO

THE JOURNEY BEFORE THE STORE

A smartphone begins long before it reaches a store shelf. The process starts with raw materials: cobalt, lithium, copper, gold, tungsten, and coltanA mineral refined into tantalum, a metal used in small electronic parts inside phones and laptops.. Many of these minerals come from countries that have been shaped by colonialism, weak governments, foreign investment, and global demand for resources. In the Democratic Republic of Congo, some mining is industrial, but some is artisanal miningSmall-scale mining where people often dig by hand using simple tools.. Workers may dig by hand for minerals that eventually become part of expensive electronics.

A smartphone begins long before it reaches a store. It starts with raw materials like cobalt, lithium, copper, gold, tungsten, and coltan. Many of these minerals come from countries shaped by colonialism, weak governments, foreign investment, and global demand. In the Democratic Republic of Congo, some mining is done by large companies. Some is artisanal mining, where people dig by hand using simple tools. Workers may dig for minerals that later become part of expensive electronics.

After mining, the parts move through a supply chainThe network of people, companies, and countries that move a product from raw material to customer. that crosses several continents. A chip may be designed in the United States, produced in Taiwan, and made with machines from the Netherlands. A memory component may come from South Korea. A screen may be made with glass technology developed in the United States and processed elsewhere. These parts travel by ship, plane, train, and truck before they become one finished device.

Final assembly often happens in enormous factory systems. At Foxconn facilities in China, workers have assembled phones, tablets, and laptops for global brands. Factory work can provide wages to people who migrated from rural areas to cities, but it can also involve long hours, strict supervision, repetitive tasks, and pressure to meet production goals. This is the human side of the global supply chain. The finished product looks smooth and simple because the labor behind it has been hidden.

CHAPTER THREE

THE PHONE BECOMES A GLOBAL NETWORK

The smartphone did more than move through globalization; it also made globalization faster. With mobile internet, people could communicate across borders instantly, follow news from other countries, send money, sell products, and organize political action. Apps turned phones into banks, maps, classrooms, cameras, marketplaces, and newsrooms. This mattered especially in places where many people skipped desktop computers and went straight to mobile internet. A device built by global trade also became a tool that connected everyday people to the global economy.

The smartphone did more than move through globalization. It also made globalization faster. With mobile internet, people could communicate across borders, follow news, send money, sell products, and organize. Apps turned phones into banks, maps, classrooms, cameras, marketplaces, and newsrooms. This mattered in places where many people never owned desktop computers. A device built by global trade became a tool that connected everyday people to the global economy.

The same network also created new dangers. Companies collected user data: location, purchases, messages, searches, videos watched, and time spent on apps. This data became valuable because it could predict behavior and sell advertising. Scholars call this surveillance capitalismA system where companies collect personal data and use it to make money, often through targeted advertising.. The user felt like the customer, but in many apps the user's attention and information became the product being sold.

Governments also used mobile technology in different ways. In democracies, activists used smartphones to record police violence, spread protest information, and organize campaigns. In authoritarian states, governments used phones to track activists, monitor speech, and shut down networks during unrest. This is why the smartphone is not simply good or bad. It is a tool of globalization, and tools can expand freedom or strengthen control depending on who uses them and who has power.

CHAPTER FOUR

THE UNEQUAL RESULTS OF CONNECTION

Globalization created real benefits. It lowered prices for many goods, spread new technology, expanded consumer choice, and helped some countries build powerful export economies. It also created new kinds of work in design, logistics, software, shipping, advertising, finance, and retail. For many people, global connection meant opportunity. The smartphone allowed people to learn, work, create, and communicate in ways that were difficult or impossible before.

Globalization created real benefits. It lowered prices for many goods. It spread new technology and gave consumers more choices. It helped some countries build strong export economies. It also created jobs in design, shipping, software, advertising, finance, and retail. For many people, global connection meant opportunity. Smartphones helped people learn, work, create, and communicate in new ways.

But the benefits were not shared equally. The people and companies that owned the brand, software, patents, and platforms often captured the most wealth. Workers who mined raw materials or assembled products usually captured much less. Some communities also faced pollution, dangerous work, and electronic waste after devices were discarded. Globalization connected the world, but connection did not automatically mean fairness.

The harder question is who gained power from that connection, and who carried the costs.

PRIMARY SOURCE MOMENT

"We get up at 6 a.m. and go to work at 7. We work until 7 p.m. and have a one-hour lunch break. After work we go to eat, and then at about 9 p.m. we go back to the dormitory to sleep. I can only call home on Sundays. Every day is like this, repeating over and over... We feel like we're like automatons."

Foxconn factory worker, interview from Shenzhen, China, 2012

WHY THIS MATTERS: This worker's account makes the hidden labor of globalization visible. The quote does not talk about technology as a miracle; it describes time, exhaustion, separation from home, and repeated motion. It helps answer the Essential Question because it shows that global connection can create useful products while also placing heavy costs on workers whose names customers never learn.

THEN AND NOW

GLOBALIZATION MAKES THE WORLD FEEL CLOSE, BUT ITS COSTS ARE OFTEN KEPT FAR AWAY.

THEN

In the 2000s, factory cities like Shenzhen became symbols of the global manufacturing system. Migrant workers left rural areas for city jobs that connected them to the world economy. Their labor helped build devices sold across the planet, but much of their work stayed invisible to consumers.

NOW

Today, people are still debating how technology should be made, repaired, reused, and recycled. Workers, journalists, repair advocates, environmental groups, and consumers have pushed companies to reveal more about supply chains and reduce waste. The modern repair movement shows agency because people are not only buying devices; they are challenging the rules that make devices disposable.

CONNECTION

The past and present connect through the same question: who controls the benefits of technology? In the early smartphone era, companies used global supply chains to make devices quickly and cheaply. Today, communities are asking whether those systems can become more transparent, more sustainable, and more fair to the people who mine, assemble, repair, and live with the effects of technology.

When you use a device every day, what responsibility do companies and consumers have to the people and places behind it?
